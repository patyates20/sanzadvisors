The part nobody puts on their website

The deal you saw on TV is not the deal that got done.

Every Shark Tank recap site still reports it as $375,000 for 25%, closed. Pat has said, repeatedly and on the record, that it isn't true — and that he's glad.

“The deal changed after the fact. It wasn't exactly the same on the show. It really wasn't anything near that.”

“We did not do the financial terms we showed on the show — we just did a relationship, a small equity portion, and no money transfer.”

“Honestly, I'm happy it didn't close at what it was before. It would have changed things for both of us — a lot more expectations on both sides.”

What Robert actually said, after taping

“You told me you didn't necessarily need the money — why do you want to do this?… Look, let's just do a small deal without the money. If you need something in the future I'll try to help you, but I want you to work with my Shark Tank group.”

They signed that instead. Pat has worked with Herjavec's team ever since — which turned out to be worth considerably more than $375,000.

As recounted by Pat Yates · The Quiet Light Podcast

Worth saying plainly: a deal was signed. Not the televised one. Anyone telling you the Shark Tank handshake is the transaction has never been through a closing — which is roughly the point of everything else on this site.

Disney. Marvel. DreamWorks. All 32 NFL teams. The NCAA, the NBA, MLB, Bluey — and a reality-TV star nobody else thought to call. HappyFeet Slippers went from a folding cart in a shopping mall to a licensing portfolio most consumer-products companies ten times its size never assemble.

Where it started

A container of slippers started it all.

Pat's father and stepmother were working mall kiosks in the mid-90s, hunting for something to sell. At the Atlanta Gift Mart they found a vendor with plush slippers and agreed to buy a container of them. That was the whole strategy.

In 2002 Pat bought them out and took it somewhere they hadn't: online. Web sales that first year were $22,000. He then scaled the seasonal dealer program from about thirty carts to roughly three hundred in four or five years — one of the larger seasonal kiosk programs in the country — while the direct site compounded behind it. By the time Shark Tank came calling the brand was in 190 retail locations across 40 states and three countries, with a catalogue that had grown from 16 SKUs to more than 5,000.

“We went from $22,000 to about $100,000, to about $400, and we capped a million — and now we're well over three million.”

Pat Yates · web sales, 2002 through 2015

What it actually takes

A major studio license is a six-figure commitment before you sell anything: an advance paid up front, a royalty on every unit, a marketing fee stacked on top, a year of product development, audits to verify what you report — and 250 to 300 SKUs bought and sitting in a warehouse on launch day.

Most people who ask Pat about licensing have no idea what the entry fee is. He's signed enough of them to describe the mechanics without flinching — which is exactly why founders now hire him to look at theirs.
